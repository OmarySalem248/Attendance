Attendance rate
The share of school days students actually attended. A rate of 90% means students attended 9 days in every 10.
Attending 90% or more
The share of students who attended at least 9 days in 10. Students below this line miss about four weeks of school a year.
Year before school
The year a child is eligible for preschool, before they start full-time school. Programs should offer 600 hours, about 15 hours a week.

Part 1

Attendance across the nation

Australian students in Years 1–10 attended 92.7% of school days in 2014. By 2025 that had fallen to 88.8%, and the decline has not been even across the country.

By state

Where students attend most

Use the slider to move through the years. The Northern Territory has the lowest attendance rate of any state or territory in every year.

By remoteness

Where attendance is highest within each state

Metropolitan areas have the highest attendance rates, with some states making exceptions in certain years. The Northern Territory and Tasmania seem to be the only territories and states where attendance is consistently higher in remote areas than in metropolitan areas. This could be due to the fact that Darwin and Hobart are the only metropolitan areas in their respective states, and they are much smaller compared to other metropolitan areas such as Sydney or Melbourne.

Below 90%

Students missing more than one day in ten

Each circle shows the share of students who attend fewer than 90% of school days. The Northern Territory has the largest share.

Part 2

Trends between sectors

Government, Catholic and Independent schools all lost ground after COVID-19, but private schools started higher and have recovered further.

By sector and gender

Private schools attend more, and the gender gap flips with the sector

Attendance rates are higher in Catholic and Independent schools than in government schools. However, across all sectors, attendance has yet to fully recover from COVID-19.

The gap between female and male students is small, and its direction depends on the sector. In general, female students attend more than male students in government schools, while male students attend more in private schools. However, between 2017 and 2022, female students in Catholic and independent schools attended more than male students, but the gap has since flipped.

Part 3

Which years show up?

Attendance depends on how old students are. It holds steady through primary school, then falls in the early years of high school. This can be attributed to the students simply having more responsibilities and distractions as they get older.

By year level

Older students attend less, and the drop starts in Year 8

On the left, each dot is one school day for the average student. On the right, each row is a year level and each column a school year. Attendance holds steady from Year 1 to Year 7, then falls sharply once students reach Year 8, the second year of high school in most states.

Every year level dipped in 2022, when Year 10 attendance fell to 82.9%. None has climbed back to its pre-COVID level: Year 10 reached 84.5% in 2025, still below 88.4% in 2019. Even after drops, attendance is still higher for Year 1 in 2025 (90.6%) than it was for Year 10 in 2014 (89.5%).

Part 4

Indigenous and non-Indigenous students

The largest gap in attendance is between Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander students and non-Indigenous students, and it is widest in remote Australia.

By Indigenous status

The gap is widening, fastest in remote areas

Since 2016, attendance has fallen in every remoteness area for both groups, but far more steeply for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander students. In very remote areas, their attendance dropped 12.2 percentage points to 54%, compared with a 4.2-point fall for non-Indigenous students.

Indigenous students by state

The Northern Territory stands apart, but Tasmania shines a light

Each circle is a state or territory, moved apart so none overlap. In the Northern Territory, nearly 4 in 10 students are Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander, and those students attend only 58% of school days. Tasmania, on the other hand, has the second-highest Indigenous share, but its Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander students attend far more often.

Choose a remoteness area to compare like with like. In very remote areas, attendance falls to 46% in the Northern Territory and around 53% in South Australia and Western Australia. There is not enough data to show rates for some areas, such as major cities in the Northern Territory.

Part 5

The very beginning: preschool

Attendance habits begin before Year 1. In the year before school, most children are enrolled in a preschool program offering at least 600 hours, about 15 hours a week, but not all of them attend that much.

Preschool attendance

Most preschoolers attend 600 hours or more, but not everywhere

Each pie is a state or territory, sized by how many children are in the chosen group and moved apart so none overlap. Slices show how many hours those children attended in the year before school. Nationally, 72% of children attended 600 hours or more.

Switch the group to see the gap widen. Only 34% of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children in the Northern Territory reached 600 hours, and 31% of disadvantaged children there, compared with more than 70% in New South Wales and Tasmania. South Australia and Western Australia also sit near 40% for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children.

Preschool by service type

Standalone preschools rarely reach 600 hours

Nationally, children whose preschool program is run inside centre-based day care almost all attend 600 hours or more (82%), as do children who use more than one service type (97%). In standalone preschools, only 31% reach 600 hours, and attendance is spread across every band.

What it means?

Recovery is needed

Even after the pandemic, attendance still lags behind pre-pandemic levels in most regions. On yop of that, many gaps still pressist in regional, sector and cultural contexts.
